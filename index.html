Pausing

Start means start: pressing it on the setup page puts the aeroplane in the air and running. After that the Pause button in the header, the one on the touch bar and the Z key are the same switch, and while it is stopped the instruments dim behind a play button you can tap anywhere on to carry on. The clearance, the readouts and the map stay readable underneath, so pausing to work something out on the map does not hide the thing you were reading.

Hold-to-turn is the default. Everything else is a tap. Nothing here needs the mouse, but the mouse works everywhere the keys do.

The four scenario buttons

New random rolls a fresh clearance and a fresh arrival — a completely new problem. Apply fields takes the radial, turns, legs, arrival and forced entry type from the boxes and builds that clearance, then gives you an arrival for it. New arrival keeps the clearance you have and drops you in from somewhere else, which is how you drill one hold from every direction. Restart this one puts you back on the exact heading and position you began with, clock and trail wiped, so you can refly an entry you got wrong and compare.

How you get to a DME fix

A DME fix is a point on a radial, and that radial is how you reach it. Nobody can navigate to fifteen miles out on the three zero five radial while tracking the one seven eight without solving a triangle in their head, so ATC does not ask: you are already on that radial, and you fly it until the DME comes up.

That means a hold at a DME fix gives you one of two arrivals, not a random one. Tracking inbound toward the station you cross the fix and the pattern is behind you, which is a direct entry. Tracking outbound from the station you arrive at the fix having come from the other side, which puts you a hundred and eighty degrees from the inbound course and gives you a teardrop or a parallel depending on which way your tracking error happens to lie. The pattern itself can be beyond the fix or between the fix and the station, and the compass point in the clearance tells you which.

Holds overhead the station are different, because you can be vectored to a VOR from any direction at all, so those still come at you from anywhere. The same goes for an RNAV waypoint, which you can go direct to from wherever you are.

Constant rate against constant radius

A standard rate turn holds the RATE at three degrees a second, so in wind the ground track is blown out of shape: fly a full circle in a twenty five knot wind and you finish nearly a mile from where you started. Constant radius holds the GROUND TRACK circular instead, varying the rate with groundspeed — faster and steeper downwind, slower and shallower upwind — and the circle closes on itself.

That is what GPS roll steering does, and it is the reason an RNAV hold keeps its shape in wind while a hand flown one has to be corrected on every leg. Fly the same hold both ways with the breadcrumbs on and the difference is obvious: the constant rate pattern walks downwind, and the constant radius one does not.

With the attitude indicator fitted you can watch the bank change round a constant radius turn. At a hundred knots in a twenty five knot wind it runs from about nine degrees upwind to twenty three degrees downwind, against a steady fifteen for a constant rate turn.

The attitude indicator

An optional vacuum gyro horizon, off by default. It is driven from the rate of turn rather than being a separate model, because in a coordinated turn the two are the same thing: the tangent of the bank equals the true airspeed times the rate of turn divided by gravity. That works out at about fifteen degrees at a hundred knots and standard rate, which is where the rule of thumb of a tenth of your airspeed plus seven comes from.

The horizon stays level, and the pitch ladder never moves off zero. That is not an omission: this simulation has no vertical dimension, and an attitude indicator that invented one would teach you to look at something that is not there. What it is genuinely useful for is establishing the bank quickly and holding it steady, which is the other half of flying an accurate leg.

The turn coordinator

Fitted by default, and it is the instrument you would actually be looking at to set up a turn. The little aeroplane banks in proportion to the rate of turn, not the angle of bank, so putting a wing tip on the doghouse is a standard rate turn whatever your airspeed — three degrees a second, a full circle in the two minutes printed on the dial, and half a turn in one. That is the whole basis of the timing in a hold.

The ball underneath is a real inclinometer, not decoration. It swings out while you are rolling into or out of a bank, because the roll is not yet matched by the turn, then settles back to centre once the turn is established. It says NO PITCH INFORMATION on the face for the same reason the real one does: this instrument tells you nothing about whether you are climbing or descending.

The menu

The app opens on a menu with the two exercises on it: holding patterns and DME arcs. Picking one opens a setup page for the clearance and the tolerances, with two ways out of it — Start rolls a random clearance inside whatever you have set, and Use these exact fields builds the one in the boxes. The controls on that page are the same ones in the Panel, borrowed for the occasion, so there is only ever one radial box and one difficulty menu to get out of step.

The setup page is a fixed heading, a scrolling middle and an action row that stays put, so Start is always where you left it however long the settings run. Given the width for it the clearance settings split into two columns, which puts the whole thing on one screen on an iPad in landscape; on a phone, or in portrait with a lot set, the middle scrolls under the buttons.

The Menu button in the header brings it back at any time and Esc steps back a page, leaving whatever you were flying exactly where it was. Switching exercises does not disturb the aeroplane: the wind, airspeed, equipment, turn model and every other setting carry straight across.

Grading

Nothing you fly will be mathematically perfect, so the question is not whether you were exact but how much of your allowance you spent. Every item is measured against a tolerance in the units you already think in — seconds on the leg, miles off the fix, fraction of full scale deflection — and scored 100 for no error at all, 70 at exactly the tolerance, and zero at twice it. Seventy is the pass mark, so a score in the seventies means legal but with nothing in hand, and the number keeps improving after you are inside tolerance because the margin you had left is the thing that actually gets better with practice.

Beside each score is the percentage of the tolerance that item consumed. That is the number to watch week to week: four seconds long on a one minute leg is 40% of the ACS allowance whether you passed or not, and watching it fall is more useful than watching a pass turn into another pass.

A hold is graded a lap at a time. The inbound leg is timed from the moment you are established — inside twenty five degrees of the inbound course — to crossing the fix, and compared with the leg you were cleared for; on an RNAV hold it is measured in miles instead. The fix crossing is how far to one side you went past. Course held is your average deviation over the inbound leg as a fraction of full scale, which is the ACS's three quarters of a dot in the form the instrument actually shows it. Non-holding side is how long you spent on the wrong side of the inbound course, counted only from the second crossing on, since a parallel entry is flown over there on purpose. The arrival at the fix is recorded but not graded: there was no leg of yours before it.

The entry is marked three ways, which is not the same question three times. Did you call one before the fix or did the simulation have to stop and ask; was it the right sector; and — the one worth having — did you fly what you called. Calling teardrop and then flying a direct is a common thing to do and nothing else will tell you that you did it. It is judged from your heading and which side of the course you are on at the first steady wings-level after the fix, and if that is genuinely ambiguous it is left unmarked rather than guessed at.

An arc is graded on the proportion of the arc you held inside tolerance rather than the average error, because one bad excursion and a generally sloppy arc average out the same and do not feel remotely the same. Then the worst excursion, where you left the arc against the computed lead radial, and how well you tracked the final course afterwards.

Difficulty

Difficulty changes the tolerances you are graded against and nothing else. The aeroplane, the wind and the clearance are identical on all three: Learning is generous, for getting the shape of the thing right before worrying about the numbers; Proficient is tighter than a first attempt and looser than the ride; and Checkride is the instrument ACS — back to the fix within ten seconds, the course within three quarters of a dot. Changing it re-grades the laps you have already flown, so a session is always measured against one set of numbers rather than a moving one.

Two honest limits on all of this. It grades what the simulation models, which is lateral navigation and timing — a real ride adds altitude, airspeed, configuration and talking to someone while you do it. And if the entry sector diagram or the ideal pattern overlay was on, the card says so, because a lap flown with the answer drawn on the map is practice and not a measurement.

DME arcs

An arc is a circle of constant DME about the station, so your own radial is your position along it and the DME window is the entire task. The clearance gives you four things: a radial to intercept it from, the arc distance, which way round to go, and the radial to turn inbound on. It is the feeder route off an approach plate, and it is flown the same way.

You start on the intercept radial, either outbound from the station or inbound to it, because a point on an arc is not somewhere you can navigate to any other way. Fly the radial until the arc distance comes up, leading the turn by about half your turn radius — a bit over a quarter of a mile at a hundred knots — and roll out ninety degrees off, with the station on a wing tip.

Then it is twist and turn. Set the OBS ten degrees ahead of yourself in the direction you are going, hold the heading, and wait for the needle to centre; when it does you have flown ten degrees round, so turn ten degrees and twist another ten. The distance corrects itself between times: outside the arc, turn toward the station; inside it, turn away. A few degrees of correction is plenty, and in wind you will find yourself holding more of it on one side of the arc than the other.

The lead radial for the turn inbound is where the turn radius subtends the station — about two degrees on a fifteen mile arc, more on a tight one, and it is drawn as an amber tick across the arc on the map. Turn inbound there and roll out on the final course; the panel then measures how well you track it in to the station.

The arc card beside the readouts shows how far off the distance you are, live, colour coded: green inside half a mile, amber to a mile, red beyond. When you finish, the verdict panel gives you the average and the worst deviation for the whole arc, where you actually started the turn inbound against where the lead radial was, and how well you held the final course. Half a mile either side is the tolerance an arc on an approach is flown to.

Choosing what you practise

Two menus in the panel decide what ATC hands you. Hold type is either a VOR hold on the clock or an RNAV hold on mileage legs, or left mixed. Pick RNAV and the clearance comes with mileage legs, which the GPS navigator flies on either head. The leg lengths on offer follow what is fitted rather than which display you are looking at: take the navigator out of the aeroplane and the mileage options grey out, because nothing on board can measure a leg in miles.

Hold fix is either overhead the station or out along the radial at a distance you set, or mixed. Out on the radial is the case a controller gives you when they say to hold fifteen miles out: the DME window still reads to the station, so that is how you find the fix, and you never touch the cone of confusion the whole lap.

Equipment

The panel has tick boxes for what is fitted: the CDI head with its directional gyro, the G5, and the GPS navigator. The navigator is wired to whichever head you have, so it is the aeroplane's RNAV capability, not the display — take it out and the mileage leg options grey out and ATC stops issuing them, whatever is on the panel. One nav display always has to stay fitted, so unticking the last one puts it straight back.

GPS / VLOC: the source is a switch, not a display

The navigator feeds whatever head is wired to it, so an ordinary CDI head flies an RNAV hold perfectly well — the deviation arriving at the OBS is simply computed rather than received. That is what the GPS/VLOC switch on the navigator does, and it is the switch that decides what the needle means. There is a VLOC / GPS selector above the readouts and the V key does the same thing.

The clearance sets it for you when it is issued: mileage legs come up on GPS, a timed VOR hold comes up on VLOC. But it is only a default, and you can leave it in the wrong place — fly an RNAV hold with the needle still on the VOR receiver and it will track the station instead of the leg, which is one of the easier ways to bust a hold for real. The CDI head annunciates the source under the face, magenta for GPS with the full scale in miles beside it, and the G5 needle goes magenta for the same reason.

What does not change with the source is the hardware. A GPS-fed CDI head is still a moving-coil pointer with mass and damping, so it lags and overshoots exactly as it does on a VOR — the signal behind it is clean, the movement in front of it is not. That is worth flying once: the same clean GPS cross-track drawn instantly on the G5 and dragged there by a mechanical needle beside it.

Green needle means you are working the station, so everything that comes with that is in play — the needle tightens without limit as you close in, and the cone of confusion still swallows it overhead. Magenta means the position is being computed, so the bar stays linear and calm straight across the fix. Same hold, same aeroplane, completely different picture.

Two panels, three behaviours

The steam gauge panel is a period one: a VOR/LOC course deviation head with its compass card driven by the OBS knob, flown beside a separate directional gyro. The card shows your selected course at the top index, not your heading, so the two instruments have to be cross-referenced — and if you set the reciprocal by mistake, the head reverse-senses exactly as the real thing does. The glideslope pointer sits parked behind its red GS flag, since a VOR is tuned. The needle is a moving-coil pointer with mass and damping: it lags, overshoots, drifts with receiver noise, and knocks against its stops. When the signal degrades a red NAV flag is pulled into the annunciator window by its spring, and the TO and FROM triangles fade out together through ambiguity.

The G5 is a renderer, not a movement. Deviation is drawn where the computed number says, with no inertia and nothing to bang against, and TO/FROM switches cleanly rather than fading. When the signal fails its validity test the deviation bar and bearing pointer are removed outright and an amber NAV annunciation appears — no gradual warning, it is either there or it is not. That difference matters over the station: the mechanical unit tells you it is losing confidence, while the digital one hands you a clean picture right up until it hands you nothing.

On an iPad

Multiple fingers work. Every control tracks the specific touch that grabbed it and ignores the others, so you can hold a turn button down with one thumb while working the OBS knob, tapping the stopwatch, or calling an entry with the other hand. Releasing one finger never releases another control.

Touch controls switch themselves on when the browser reports a coarse pointer, and there is a checkbox in the Flight panel to force them either way if you want to see them on a desktop. They bring up a control bar along the bottom: hold Left or Right to turn, tap Speed up to run the clock fast and tap it again to drop back, and the number beside it cycles the multiplier.

The stopwatch works the way a stopwatch does. Tap to start, tap to stop, tap the stopped clock again to zero it. That applies both to the bar and to the timer card on the panel. Everything else is already touchable: drag round the OBS knob on the CDI head or the G5 encoder, press the G5 encoder to swap between HDG and CRS, drag the DG to move the heading bug, and double tap the DG knob to realign the gyro.

The map moves with the layout. On a desktop it sits in the side rail; on a tablet, where the rail is a slide-over that would bury it, it moves into the stage itself — under the readouts in landscape, below them in portrait. It is the same element and the same canvas either way, so every layer toggle comes with it. Hide and Show on the map header collapses it when you want the space back, which is the same thing the M key does.

In landscape the instrument moves to its own column on the left with the clearance, readouts and entry buttons stacked beside it, so the gauge gets the full height of the screen. Below about 1,200 points wide the side panel becomes a slide-over — the Panel button in the header opens it and Close panel puts it away. Added to the Home Screen the app runs full screen with no browser chrome, and it asks for a screen wake lock so the iPad will not dim on you halfway round a lap.

Working the G5

The real unit has exactly one control: a push-and-turn rotary encoder at the lower right, and this one behaves the same way. Press it to swap which field the knob is editing, and the selected field is shown highlighted at the top of the display, the way Garmin marks a value that is live for editing. Then turn the knob to change it. Rolling the wheel anywhere over the unit turns the knob too, one degree a detent and ten with Shift held, and the OBS and heading bug keys keep working independently of whichever field the knob happens to be on.

What ATC is actually giving you

Two things in the clearance decide how you fly the pattern. A hold over the station or at a DME fix is a timed hold — one minute legs below 14,000, one and a half above — and you fly it on the clock. Legs given in miles are an RNAV hold, because measuring a leg by distance needs GPS; the clock stops mattering and you turn when the distance comes up. The panel under the clearance tells you which one you have been handed.

The fix does not have to be the station. Set a DME distance and the hold moves out along the radial, exactly as when a controller says to hold fifteen miles out on the two two six radial. The DME window still reads to the VOR, so fifteen DME is how you identify the fix, and the map shows it as a triangle on the radial with the station still drawn back at the origin. Everything else is unchanged: the inbound course is still the reciprocal of the radial and the pattern still lies on the far side. One useful side effect is that a hold out at a DME fix never puts you in the cone of confusion, so the needle stays honest all the way across.

Heading against track

The magenta triangle riding the rim of the G5 rose is your ground track: the direction the aeroplane is actually travelling once the wind has had its say. In still air it sits under the lubber line and you can forget about it. Put a crosswind in and it slides off to one side, and the gap between it and the top of the card is your drift, read straight off the instrument instead of worked out. Park the track pointer on the inbound course and you are tracking the course, whatever heading that takes.

That is the whole wind correction problem made visible, which is worth a moment before you go back to the steam gauges — the CDI head cannot tell you any of this, and inferring it from the way the needle creeps is exactly the skill the older panel demands.

VOR against GPS

A VOR measures an angle, so its deviation is a fixed number of degrees however far out you are, and it tightens without limit as you close on the station. GPS measures cross-track distance, so full scale is a fixed number of miles — 2.0 enroute, 1.0 terminal, 0.30 on approach — and the bar behaves the same a mile from the fix as it does thirty. Fly the same hold on VOR and then on GPS — on either head — and watch what happens overhead: the VOR bar becomes unusable and flags, while the GPS bar tracks straight through, flipping TO to FROM cleanly at the abeam line. There is no cone of confusion on GPS because nothing is being measured from the station.

Station passage

The cone of confusion is modelled with a half-angle of 30° from the vertical, so it begins once the station climbs above about 60° of elevation and its radius is a little over half your height above the station. At 4,000 ft that is roughly 0.4 NM, matching the usual rule of thumb of half a mile at 6,000 ft and three miles at FL360. Both the altitude and the half-angle are adjustable in the Flight panel.

Much of what you see near the station is not the cone at all. A VOR measures angles, so at half a mile out full-scale deflection is under 100 yards of lateral offset — the needle gets hypersensitive purely from geometry, before any signal degradation. That part is in the model whatever you set the cone to. The first positive indication of station passage is the first complete reversal of the TO/FROM indication, not the needle.

Reading the HSI and the CDI head

Both are scaled for VOR. The G5 shows two dots either side at 5° a dot; the mechanical head shows five at 2°. Full deflection is 10° on both. The magenta needle on the mechanical panel and the cyan one on the G5 are bearing pointers to the station.

How the timing works

Outbound timing starts abeam the fix, or wings level outbound if you never get an abeam. Inbound timing starts wings level on the inbound course. If the inbound leg is not the length you were cleared for, adjust the outbound leg by the difference on the next lap.

With wind, apply roughly triple the inbound drift correction on the outbound leg, and shorten or lengthen the outbound time to make the inbound leg come out right. Turn on the gyro drift setting if you want the mechanical panel to make you work for the heading as well.
