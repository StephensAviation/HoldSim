The four scenario buttons

New random rolls a fresh clearance and a fresh arrival — a completely new problem. Apply fields takes the radial, turns, legs, arrival and forced entry type from the boxes and builds that clearance, then gives you an arrival for it. New arrival keeps the clearance you have and drops you in from somewhere else, which is how you drill one hold from every direction. Restart this one puts you back on the exact heading and position you began with, clock and trail wiped, so you can refly an entry you got wrong and compare.

Three indicators, three behaviours

The steam gauge option is a period panel: a VOR/LOC course deviation head with its compass card driven by the OBS knob, flown beside a separate directional gyro. The card shows your selected course at the top index, not your heading, so the two instruments have to be cross-referenced — and if you set the reciprocal by mistake, the head reverse-senses exactly as the real thing does. The glideslope pointer sits parked behind its red GS flag, since a VOR is tuned. The needle is a moving-coil pointer with mass and damping: it lags, overshoots, drifts with receiver noise, and knocks against its stops. When the signal degrades a red NAV flag is pulled into the annunciator window by its spring, and the TO and FROM triangles fade out together through ambiguity.

The G5 is a renderer, not a movement. Deviation is drawn where the computed number says, with no inertia and nothing to bang against, and TO/FROM switches cleanly rather than fading. When the signal fails its validity test the deviation bar and bearing pointer are removed outright and an amber NAV annunciation appears — no gradual warning, it is either there or it is not. That difference matters over the station: the mechanical unit tells you it is losing confidence, while the digital one hands you a clean picture right up until it hands you nothing.

On an iPad

Touch controls switch themselves on when the browser reports a coarse pointer, and there is a checkbox in the Flight panel to force them either way if you want to see them on a desktop. They bring up a control bar along the bottom: hold Left or Right to turn, tap Speed up to run the clock fast and tap it again to drop back, and the number beside it cycles the multiplier.

The stopwatch works the way a stopwatch does. Tap to start, tap to stop, tap the stopped clock again to zero it. That applies both to the bar and to the timer card on the panel. Everything else is already touchable: drag round the OBS knob on the CDI head or the G5 encoder, press the G5 encoder to swap between HDG and CRS, drag the DG to move the heading bug, and double tap the DG knob to realign the gyro.

The map moves with the layout. On a desktop it sits in the side rail; on a tablet, where the rail is a slide-over that would bury it, it moves into the stage itself — under the readouts in landscape, below them in portrait. It is the same element and the same canvas either way, so every layer toggle comes with it. Hide and Show on the map header collapses it when you want the space back, which is the same thing the M key does.

In landscape the instrument moves to its own column on the left with the clearance, readouts and entry buttons stacked beside it, so the gauge gets the full height of the screen. Below about 1,200 points wide the side panel becomes a slide-over — the Panel button in the header opens it and Close panel puts it away. Added to the Home Screen the app runs full screen with no browser chrome, and it asks for a screen wake lock so the iPad will not dim on you halfway round a lap.

Working the G5

The real unit has exactly one control: a push-and-turn rotary encoder at the lower right, and this one behaves the same way. Press it to swap which field the knob is editing, and the selected field is shown highlighted at the top of the display, the way Garmin marks a value that is live for editing. Then turn the knob to change it. Rolling the wheel anywhere over the unit turns the knob too, one degree a detent and ten with Shift held, and the OBS and heading bug keys keep working independently of whichever field the knob happens to be on.

What ATC is actually giving you

Two things in the clearance decide how you fly the pattern. A hold over the station or at a DME fix is a timed hold — one minute legs below 14,000, one and a half above — and you fly it on the clock. Legs given in miles are an RNAV hold, because measuring a leg by distance needs GPS; the clock stops mattering and you turn when the distance comes up. The panel under the clearance tells you which one you have been handed.

The fix does not have to be the station. Set a DME distance and the hold moves out along the radial, exactly as when a controller says to hold fifteen miles out on the two two six radial. The DME window still reads to the VOR, so fifteen DME is how you identify the fix, and the map shows it as a triangle on the radial with the station still drawn back at the origin. Everything else is unchanged: the inbound course is still the reciprocal of the radial and the pattern still lies on the far side. One useful side effect is that a hold out at a DME fix never puts you in the cone of confusion, so the needle stays honest all the way across.

VOR against GPS

A VOR measures an angle, so its deviation is a fixed number of degrees however far out you are, and it tightens without limit as you close on the station. GPS measures cross-track distance, so full scale is a fixed number of miles — 2.0 enroute, 1.0 terminal, 0.30 on approach — and the bar behaves the same a mile from the fix as it does thirty. Fly the same hold on G5 VOR and then on G5 GPS and watch what happens overhead: the VOR bar becomes unusable and flags, while the GPS bar tracks straight through, flipping TO to FROM cleanly at the abeam line. There is no cone of confusion on GPS because nothing is being measured from the station.

Station passage

The cone of confusion is modelled with a half-angle of 30° from the vertical, so it begins once the station climbs above about 60° of elevation and its radius is a little over half your height above the station. At 4,000 ft that is roughly 0.4 NM, matching the usual rule of thumb of half a mile at 6,000 ft and three miles at FL360. Both the altitude and the half-angle are adjustable in the Flight panel.

Much of what you see near the station is not the cone at all. A VOR measures angles, so at half a mile out full-scale deflection is under 100 yards of lateral offset — the needle gets hypersensitive purely from geometry, before any signal degradation. That part is in the model whatever you set the cone to. The first positive indication of station passage is the first complete reversal of the TO/FROM indication, not the needle.

Reading the HSI and the CDI head

Both are scaled for VOR. The G5 shows two dots either side at 5° a dot; the mechanical head shows five at 2°. Full deflection is 10° on both. The magenta needle on the mechanical panel and the cyan one on the G5 are bearing pointers to the station.

How the timing works

Outbound timing starts abeam the fix, or wings level outbound if you never get an abeam. Inbound timing starts wings level on the inbound course. If the inbound leg is not the length you were cleared for, adjust the outbound leg by the difference on the next lap.

With wind, apply roughly triple the inbound drift correction on the outbound leg, and shorten or lengthen the outbound time to make the inbound leg come out right. Turn on the gyro drift setting if you want the mechanical panel to make you work for the heading as well.
